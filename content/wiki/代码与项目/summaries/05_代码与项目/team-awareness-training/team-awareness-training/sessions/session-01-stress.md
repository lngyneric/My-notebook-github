# Session #1 - An overview of stress
## Session Overview
In this session, participants will learn the basic theoretical framework of what causes stress, how stress activates in the physical body, why stress works in such a way, how stress relates to productivity and a simple method to battle stress - deep breathing.

## Session Structure & Timing
Total session flow is structured as follows, with a required teacher note to document all key findings and participant questions for follow-up via post-session email:
1. [Introduction](#1-introduction): Teacher and participant introductions, course goal alignment (10 min)
2. [Teaching: An Overview of Stress](#2-teaching-an-overview-of-stress): Core theory of stress origins, mechanisms and impacts (20 min)
3. [Method: Deep Breathing](#3-method-deep-breathing): Guided practice of the core stress intervention (20 min)
4. [Reflection & Homework](#4-reflection): Individual reflection and practice accountability planning (10 min)

---

## 1. Introduction
1. **Teacher Introduction**: Present your background and motivation for leading the course.
2. **Participant Introductions**: Conduct a clockwise round-robin where participants share their daily routine and goals for the course. Use mnemonics or written notes to retain participant names.
3. **Offline Session Agreement**: Facilitate a group decision on banning digital devices during the session. Prior participants report this practice is surprisingly calming; frame the option as a temporary experiment if the group is hesitant, with a final decision after the first session.

## 2. Teaching: An Overview of Stress
### Core Stress Properties
Stress has a dual nature: mild stress is beneficial as it maintains alertness, but excessive stress impairs focus, increases unproductive multitasking, and reduces overall productivity. Chronic stress is linked to physical health conditions including cardiovascular disease and psoriasis, and can lead to burnout.
- Most people can identify common external stress triggers (too much or too little work, lack of control, missing feedback loops, no sense of purpose) so this material is typically not prioritized.
- Maladaptive coping for unmanaged workday stress (called stress self-medication) includes use of alcohol, smoking, marijuana, pornography, and sugar to temporarily improve mood.

### Evolutionary Origins of Stress
Participants typically engage most with the evolutionary basis of stress:
- The **amygdala** is the brain region responsible for identifying and reacting to danger. Prehistoric humans with stronger amygdala function were better able to avoid immediate lethal threats (physical danger, tribal exclusion, which was often fatal for isolated individuals) and thus survived to pass on this trait.
- This creates an evolutionary stress mismatch in modern contexts (2015 framing): abstract large-scale threats like climate change do not trigger the amygdala's fear response, but minor personal stressors (e.g. a failing work project) do activate the full stress response.

### Fight-Flight-or-Freeze Response
The amygdala triggers the physical *fight-flight-or-freeze* stress response, adapted for physical danger: it increases heart rate, induces sweating, dilates pupils, and heightens all senses to improve survival odds.
- This response is maladaptive for modern workplaces: constant overstimulation from open office layouts and digital notifications amplifies stress, creating a self-reinforcing stress-productivity feedback loop: high stress reduces focus and sense of progress, which increases stress further, which impairs sleep, leading to even higher stress levels.
- A framing analogy: human brains are "buggy", optimized for the prehistoric "operating system" rather than modern work environments.

## 3. Method: Deep Breathing
Deep breathing is the simplest evidence-based intervention to counteract the fight-flight-or-freeze response.
### Core Technique Guidance
- Place one hand on the stomach: expand the stomach on the in-breath, and collapse it on the out-breath.
- Maintain good posture (straight back) for optimal effect.
### Supplementary Practice Tools
Participants are encouraged to test which tools work best for their individual practice:
- Smiling during practice
- Breathing through the nose
- Counting breaths sequentially (1 on inhale, 2 on exhale, up to 10, then restart)
- Holding each breath for 4 seconds
- Listening to calming music
### Homework & Group Practice
- **Homework requirement**: Practice deep breathing for at least 10 minutes twice per day, plus ad-hoc practice in daily moments (e.g. waiting in coffee shop lines, in elevators).
- Facilitate a 10-minute group guided deep breathing practice, followed by open Q&A on the technique.

## 4. Reflection
1. Conduct a round-robin of individual, unfiltered reflection (not group discussion) on session learnings.
2. Prompt participants to identify specific times they will complete their daily deep breathing homework, emphasizing that practical consistent practice (not theoretical learning) is required to see results.
3. After the reflection round, inform participants you will remain available after the session for 1:1 questions not covered during group time.

## Extra Material (for unused session time)
If additional time is available, cover these optional topics:
1. **Yerkes-Dodson Graph**: A graphical model depicting the bell-curve relationship between stress and productivity, where the shape of the curve varies based on the complexity and nature of the activity being performed.
2. **Stress Response Reframing**: The physical symptoms of stress are identical to the physical symptoms of courage; intentionally smiling during stress and actively reframing the response as a helpful preparatory reaction can stop negative anxiety spirals.
3. **Pranayama Context**: The deep breathing practice taught is a form of *pranayama*, the yogic practice of breath control, which is one of the 8 limbs of traditional yoga alongside postures (asanas), ethics, and meditation.
